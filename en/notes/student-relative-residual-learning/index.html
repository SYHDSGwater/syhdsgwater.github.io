How to Fine-Tune an Already Strong SWE Agent

For a mature coding-agent policy, the best training data is not merely correct. It contains a learnable residual that the student will need in states it actually visits.

Training a weak SWE agent is a familiar problem: collect correct trajectories, remove bad actions, and run supervised fine-tuning. Training an agent that is already strong is a different problem.

Traditional filtering asks: Is this a good trajectory?

We also need to ask: Does this good trajectory still contain anything new for this student?

The argument of this note is that data selection for heavily post-trained, behaviorally mature SWE agents should move from teacher-centric quality filtering toward student-relative residual learning. Data quality is no longer only an intrinsic property of a trajectory. It is a property of the interaction between the data, the current student, and the states that student will actually visit.

SWE-Lego- and SWE-Master-style filtering remains a necessary first stage. After it, teacher-forced NLL is a useful and inexpensive second-stage signal for estimating how much information a candidate trajectory still contains for the student. The next step is to select supervision from student rollouts, student failure states, and student-relative issue difficulty, bringing the training distribution closer to deployment.

Quality filtering asks whether a trajectory is worth teaching to any model. Residual learning asks which parts are still worth teaching to this student.

#1. Why we believed in SFT

Recent SWE-agent research offers a remarkably consistent lesson: SFT is often the most cost-effective stage of capability growth for a small model.

Using high-quality data, erroneous-action masking, and a difficulty curriculum, SWE-Lego reaches 42.2% and 52.6% on SWE-bench Verified with Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B. TTS@16 raises those scores to 49.6% and 58.8%.1 Orchard's Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking reaches 64.3% after SFT and 67.5% after RL; in that setting, most of the capability is established by SFT and RL contributes the final few points.2 SWE-Master similarly combines teacher rollout, data cleaning, long-horizon SFT, execution-feedback RL, and inference scaling in a single pipeline.3

These results established today's teacher-centric recipe:

  1. sample issues from pools such as SWE-rebench, SWE-smith, and Scale-SWE;
  2. run a strong teacher in a real container;
  3. retain trajectories that pass tests and protocol checks;
  4. mask incorrect or inefficient actions and organize the data by difficulty or length;
  5. add RL or test-time scaling after SFT.13

For a weak student, this works well because the set of correct teacher trajectories overlaps heavily with the set of behaviors the student has not learned. For a strong base agent, those sets separate: the fact that a teacher can execute a trajectory does not imply that the student cannot, and the fact that the student fails does not imply that a full teacher trajectory is the most learnable form of supervision.

#2. The turn: a strong model may not need your SFT data

TMax provides an unusually clean contrast. With the same TMax-SFT data and a similar recipe, an older Qwen3-8B improves, while the heavily post-trained Qwen3.5-9B degrades. On Terminal Bench Lite, Qwen3-8B moves from 7.3% to 11.5%; Qwen3.5-9B moves from 41.9% down to 35.5%. TMax argues that Qwen3.5 already has a mature terminal-agent policy, so the new information in weaker-teacher imitation data cannot compensate for policy drift.4

We computed assistant-token teacher-forced NLL on the same 1,000 TMax-SFT trajectories using each model's official chat template:

Qwen3.5-9B0.2740.11441.9 → 35.5

Token NLL differs by 2.89×; bits per byte, which controls for tokenizer granularity, differs by 2.84×. The TMax data is clearly novel for Qwen3-8B and already highly familiar to Qwen3.5-9B. The NLL difference aligns with the opposite SFT outcomes.4

NLL asks a student-relative question

NLL does not ask whether a trajectory is correct. It asks how difficult it is for the student to predict the next reasoning segment, action, and stop signal, given the correct history produced by the teacher.

NLL⁡(D∣M)=−1N∑t=1Nlog⁡pM(yt∣x,y<t)\operatorname{NLL}(D\mid M)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log p_M(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})NLL(D∣M)=−N1​t=1∑N​logpM​(yt​∣x,y<t​)

Very low NLL means that most target tokens lie in a familiar region of the student's policy. Higher NLL may indicate unlearned behavior—but it can also come from incompatible formatting, an inexpressible teacher policy, or noise. It belongs after quality filtering, not in place of it.

#3. What SFT actually learns

Agentic SFT does not directly optimize issue resolution. It maximizes the likelihood of the teacher's next reasoning segment, tool call, and termination action on histories visited by the teacher:

LSFT=−Eh∼dT, a∼πT(⋅∣h)[log⁡πθ(a∣h)].\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}} =-\mathbb{E}_{h\sim d_T,\,a\sim\pi_T(\cdot\mid h)} \left[\log \pi_\theta(a\mid h)\right].LSFT​=−Eh∼dT​,a∼πT​(⋅∣h)​[logπθ​(a∣h)].

Here, dTd_TdT​ is the state distribution induced by the teacher interacting with a particular harness. On a fixed dataset, cross-entropy is the empirical form of minimizing DKL(πT∥πθ)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_T\Vert\pi_\theta)DKL​(πT​∥πθ​)—forward KL—but only at states h∼dTh\sim d_Th∼dT​. At deployment, the student instead sees histories h∼dπθh\sim d_{\pi_\theta}h∼dπθ​​ induced by its own previous actions. One bad search, edit, or validation decision can move it into a history never covered by the teacher data, after which errors compound along the trajectory. This is the classical distribution-shift problem in imitation learning.19

SFT can therefore strengthen an agent through three mechanisms:

  1. Interface and state alignment. The model learns to read observations and use the harness for search, editing, testing, and submission.
  2. Action-prior reshaping. Useful localization, modification, validation, and stopping patterns become more likely; invalid actions become less likely.
  3. Reusable procedure transfer. The model abstracts search and repair programs that generalize across issues.

SFT fails when the student has already learned the first two mechanisms, when a teacher's long-horizon procedure exceeds the student's expressive capacity, or when training only covers clean teacher states and never teaches recovery from the student's own mistakes. A falling loss proves that the student is becoming more like the teacher. It does not prove that the student is better at resolving issues under its own induced state distribution.

For a strong agent, the goal is not to reproduce the teacher policy in full, but to learn a useful and absorbable behavioral residual between the current student and the target policy:

training value≈correctness×residual information×learnability×target relevance×student-state coverage.\text{training value} \approx \text{correctness} \times \text{residual information} \times \text{learnability} \times \text{target relevance} \times \text{student-state coverage}.training value≈correctness×residual information×learnability×target relevance×student-state coverage.

NLL measures residual information. It does not, by itself, measure learnability or state coverage.

#4. v14.d: a 16K dataset that looked healthy

SFT v14.d used successful MiniMax-M2.5 trajectories from Orchard-SWE. The final set contains 16,124 trajectories covering 10,551 manifest issues: 72.8% from Scale-SWE and 27.2% from SWE-rebench. Filtering required external resolution, a valid protocol, no Git-history leakage, no test contamination, and no explicit unresolved self-test failure. Training retained full thinking and multi-turn observations.

By conventional standards, this was ideal data: real issues, real execution, a strong teacher, successful trajectories, and full long-horizon context. Orchard had already shown that the same broader data and framework can improve the Qwen3 family.2

We ran full-parameter SFT on Qwen3.5-4B with 64K context, two epochs, global batch size 32, and a peak learning rate of 1e-5. The complete 1,008-step W&B history is numerically healthy: mean update loss is 0.243, the final 50-step mean is 0.214, held-out loss falls from 0.2646 to 0.2541, and all recorded loss and gradient-norm values are finite.5

SFT v14.d training log

Independent teacher forcing agrees. The base model has token NLL 0.307 on v14.d train-300; after SFT it is 0.211. On validation-256, NLL falls from 0.300 to 0.252.15

Closed-loop behavior did not improve. After repairing the old evaluation template—which had omitted historical assistant reasoning from later context—the final merged 500-problem result passes 34.2%. Only 53.6% of trajectories actively submit, and 40.6% consume the full 250-turn budget. The model learned the teacher-forced objective while continuing to wander and fail to converge.6

Evaluation scope. The 34.2% is the project's final merged result. The first 200 problems include selective reruns, so it is not a single unbiased pass@1 run. The uniformly configured problems 301–500 pass at 30.0%. This limits leaderboard comparability, but does not remove the behavioral evidence in the low submit rate and high turn-cap rate.6

A resolved trajectory is a policy the teacher can execute, not necessarily one a 4B student can execute reliably. A strong teacher can explore for a long time and eventually repair the issue; a small student may copy the exploratory style without reproducing the full localization–edit–validation–termination chain.

The first two trajectory families feed successful Qwen3-8B recipes. SWE-Lego gains 34.6 points with SFT alone; Klear-AgentForge-8B-SFT gains 30.2 points.1 Under one scorer they land at 0.738 and 0.812. Together with TMax's 0.792 and positive SFT result, the evidence has moved from a cross-model directional pattern toward a same-backbone cluster. Truncation does not create the ordering: restricting the calculation to wholly untruncated trajectories gives 0.758 and 0.824.

This is still not a causal curve. The 500 examples are samples from each public SWE trajectory family, not either complete training mixture; the recipes also differ in data composition, masking, curriculum, and agent harness. Most importantly, AgentForge at 0.812 sits just above 0.8. The defensible conclusion is therefore a candidate operating region with a 0.5–0.8 core and a soft upper edge near 0.85, not a hard cutoff at 0.8. “The 0.5–0.8 golden range” remains a hypothesis to test, not a law established by these observations.

These numbers do not imply a universal NLL threshold. GRAPE selects, among multiple correct responses to an instruction, the response with higher student likelihood and outperforms blindly imitating the strongest teacher. Distributional fit is part of training value.17 Yet selecting only the lowest NLL favors content the student can already reproduce. RSR refines the picture: effective reasoning trajectories tend to contain sufficient absolute surprisal while their important tokens still rank highly under the student. They are novel without leaving the student's learnable behavioral neighborhood.18

We therefore treat the neighborhood around 0.5–0.8 only as an empirical range worth covering in a future pilot, not a decision rule. A stronger candidate criterion is: after correctness and process-quality gating, prefer trajectories with moderately elevated NLL, reasonable token rank, and a clear relationship to actual student failure modes. The range moves with model, tokenizer, template, mask, and domain; cross-tokenizer comparisons should report BPB or nats per character.

AxiaoDBL provides directionally consistent evidence. Its public log has a first-50-update mean of 0.578 and an epoch-one mean near 0.525, above v14.d's roughly 0.281 and 0.270. With only 2,784 training trajectories and two epochs, it reports 55.0% on SWE-bench Verified, +10.2 points over the model-card base score of 44.8%.7

This is not an apples-to-apples token-NLL comparison. AxiaoDBL's mean loss over the full run is 0.438, and its action mask, curriculum, and reduction differ. But an early or first-epoch signal near 0.55 remains useful directional evidence during a low-cost preflight.

#6. The rollout teacher can matter more than the issue source

The AxiaoDBL author states that its issue pool is a subset of PrimeIntellect/SWE-Lego-Real-Data-Verified and that the rollout teacher is DeepSeek-V4-Flash.79 Prime derives from SWE-rebench and repairs test IDs before validating F2P/P2P with gold patches: a strong traditional quality filter.

Issue source cannot explain the entire difference. Exact instance-ID overlap between the v14.d manifest and the 4,323 resolved Prime Verified issues gives:

Overlap measureCount / share
Exact overlapping issues1,736

Matching v14.d trajectories2,635 / 16,124 (16.3%)

The two datasets do not inhabit entirely different issue worlds. A larger difference is the behavior generated on similar issues: MiniMax-M2.5 versus DeepSeek-V4-Flash GA/0731, followed by different action masking and curriculum choices.16

The exact checkpoint matters. DeepSeek reports that V4-Flash-0731 retains the architecture and scale of Preview but receives new post-training. Terminal Bench rises from 61.8 to 82.7, NL2Repo from 39.4 to 54.2, DeepSWE from 7.3 to 54.4, and Toolathlon from 49.7 to 70.3.10

The SWE-Router DeepSeek-V4-Flash SBV traces were generated before the 0731 release and belong to the Preview period.12 Our Qwen3.5-4B NLL of 0.355 on those traces cannot substitute for AxiaoDBL's GA-teacher data. Community information identifies the AxiaoDBL teacher as the GA/0731-era model, although the public discussion retains only the mutable DeepSeek-V4-Flash alias and not an immutable revision hash.8

The unit of an agentic dataset is not an issue or even a resolved trajectory. It is issue × exact teacher checkpoint × harness × reasoning effort × sampling × loss mask.

The cheapest test is not the teacher name or its benchmark score. It is the student's likelihood on the exact candidate traces.

#7. From teacher quality to a student-relative residual

SWE-Lego improves trajectory quality through execution verification, erroneous-action masking, and difficulty curricula. SWE-Master combines reward, format, length, BoN difficulty, and LLM-judge filters. Orchard adds credit assignment that recovers valuable segments from failed trajectories.13

A strong student needs five dimensions of selection:

DimensionQuestionCandidate signals
CorrectnessDid the patch actually resolve the issue?F2P/P2P, gold-patch validation
Process qualityAre actions wrong, inefficient, or contaminated?action mask, format/length filters, LLM judge
Issue difficultyIs the issue too easy, intermediate, or impossible for this student?student pass@k, IFD
Student-relative informationIs this behavior already familiar?NLL, BPB, token rank, RSR, self–teacher gap
Deployment-state relevanceDoes supervision cover states the student reaches?student rollout prefixes, failure states, behavior classes

Correctness and process quality decide whether the data can be learned safely. The remaining dimensions decide whether this student should spend updates on it. DEITA, SWE-Lego, and SWE-Master build cleaner teacher pools; IFD estimates student-relative instruction difficulty; LESS estimates target-task relevance through gradient influence; GRAPE and RSR add distributional fit and informative alignment.1172123

NLL can remove data that is absolutely correct but relatively redundant. Token rank and execution checks help reject data that is surprising because it is wrong or unreachable. Student rollout states determine whether supervision corresponds to mistakes the deployed model will actually make.

This is student-relative residual learning:

Do not compress the strongest teacher's entire behavior into the student. Within verified behavior, teach the learnable residual between the student's current policy and the target policy.

#8. Why the data should become approximately on-policy

Pure teacher rollout is off-policy. The teacher chooses which files to inspect, which commands to run, and which intermediate states to enter. The student imitates only on those states. A resolved patch does not guarantee that the teacher states are where the student most needs help. With a long SWE-agent horizon, one early divergence can make the second half of a teacher trajectory irrelevant to the student's actual history.

DAgger queries an expert on states induced by the learner. GKD similarly lets a language-model student generate sequences first, then obtains teacher supervision on those student-generated sequences; it can also replace fixed forward-KL distillation with other objectives.19 Fully on-policy expert annotation is expensive in a real SWE environment, but several approximations are practical:

  1. rollout the student first and select issues that are intermittently solvable or have recoverable failures;
  2. start teacher correction from a student failure prefix, bad edit, or validation loop instead of restarting from a clean state;
  3. retain correct student actions and supervise only the teacher's key residual actions;
  4. recompute NLL and rank after intermediate checkpoints so that the selected data moves with the student policy.

These methods do not eliminate every limitation of forward KL. They shift the state distribution in the training expectation from dTd_TdT​ toward dπθd_{\pi_\theta}dπθ​​: the model learns at places it will actually reach, and the supervision concentrates on gaps in its current policy. For an already strong agent, that is closer to capability extension than simply enlarging a static dataset of teacher successes.

#9. A cheap preflight before training

Before paying for thousands of rollouts or a full SFT run:

  1. Apply traditional quality filters first. Require an executable environment, resolved patch, valid F2P/P2P, no leakage, and valid actions.
  2. Freeze the scoring protocol. Use the exact student checkpoint, training tokenizer/chat template, complete historical reasoning, and the same loss mask.
  3. Score 100–1,000 candidate trajectories. Report token-weighted mean, per-trajectory quantiles, target-token count, and BPB across tokenizers.
  4. Anchor against student rollouts. A teacher NLL close to the student's self-rollout NLL may contain little new information; a moderately higher value with reasonable rank and verified behavior is more promising.
  5. Estimate student-relative difficulty. Divide issues into always-pass, mixed, and always-fail using pass@k; inspect mixed and recoverable failures first.
  6. Decompose by behavior. Score reasoning, tool actions, first effective edit, verification, and submission separately.
  7. Run a small closed-loop pilot. Track pass@1, submit rate, first-edit turn, empty patch, no-progress turns, and turn/context caps before scaling.

The inexpensive signal set is therefore: pass@k for prior student capability; NLL and token rank for novelty and absorbability; student rollouts for the deployment state distribution; and a small closed-loop pilot for whether teacher-forcing gains transfer into real behavior.

#10. Next step — Coming soon

The next experiments will test the residual-learning hypothesis directly rather than comparing more teacher names.

Experiment 1: Is NLL sufficient, or is informative alignment required? With one Qwen chat template, full historical reasoning, and one loss mask, decompose assistant targets into reasoning, tool name/arguments, edits, tests/verification, and submission/termination. Compute NLL, token rank, and RSR for each category.

Experiment 2: Bucket quality-controlled data. On execution-verified trajectories from one issue pool, use <0.5, 0.5–0.8, 0.8–1.1, and >1.1 NLL bins, then match assistant-token budget, repository, length, and teacher across bins. Split each bin again by low versus high token rank. This directly tests whether 0.5–0.8 is a stable operating region, a soft boundary, or an accident of the current small evidence set.

Experiment 3: Add approximately on-policy issue and state selection. Use student pass@k to split always-pass, mixed, and always-fail issues; add teacher corrections that begin from student failure prefixes. Compare static teacher-success SFT, student-difficulty filtering, and failure-state residual SFT.

Experiment 4: Measure capability, not only loss. Hold issue count, assistant target tokens, repository distribution, steps, and teacher constant. Measure invalid tool calls, localization and first effective edit, reproduction/regression tests, premature submission, turn/context caps, checkpoint-level ID/OOD results, and cross-harness behavior.

A falsifiable prediction follows: after quality gating, medium-high-NLL, low-rank trajectories originating from mixed or failed student states will improve closed-loop performance more than lowest-NLL, highest-NLL, or random data. The gain should first appear in the student's existing failure modes—not merely in formatting or response length.

#Conclusion

Traditional SWE data filtering remains essential, but it only ensures that a trajectory is good. Extending an already strong SWE agent also requires asking whether the trajectory is informative for this student, learnable by it, and relevant to states it will actually visit.

TMax shows that the same SFT data can strengthen Qwen3-8B and weaken Qwen3.5-9B; NLL and BPB expose their very different familiarity with the data. v14.d shows that 16K rigorously filtered successful trajectories and steadily improving train and validation losses can still fail to produce a better closed-loop policy. AxiaoDBL suggests that fewer trajectories—when fresher for the student, better matched to the teacher, and paired with action-level filtering—can deliver more.

The objective should not be merely:

Find more correct trajectories.

It should become:

Within correct trajectories, find the part this student has not learned, can learn, and will need during its own rollout.

Data engineering for strong agents should continue moving from teacher-centric quality filtering toward student-relative residual learning. NLL is not the final answer, but it is cheap and direct. Combined with token rank, student difficulty, and student-generated states, it is closer to the quantity we actually care about than teacher reputation, issue source, or training loss alone.

#Method notes

  • The repaired v14.d evaluation preserves all historical assistant reasoning. Assistant turns include malformed action responses. Teacher-forced NLL targets assistant reasoning, tool-call markup, and assistant end tokens; system, user, and tool responses are context only.
  • Claude Opus 4.7 SBV traces have anomalously low assistant-turn and token counts and are excluded from the argument because the cause may be the harness or capture process.
  • Kimi-K2.6 and DeepSeek Preview NLLs measure Qwen3.5-4B familiarity with candidate trace distributions; they are not causal SFT results.12
  • All three Qwen3-8B random/resolved-500 measurements use the official Qwen3 chat template and target only complete assistant bodies, tool calls, and end tokens. SWE-Lego and Open-SWE have high truncation rates at 40,959 tokens, so the article also reports a non-truncated-trajectory sensitivity result.25
  • GRAPE and RSR are validated on general instruction tuning and reasoning-trajectory selection. Here they motivate a hypothesis for SWE-agent data selection rather than serve as existing SWE causal evidence.17
  • Figure design uses direct labels, low-saturation color, light grids, and whitespace inspired by Transformer Circuits research articles.14
